Understand migration

Migration, racism and the evidence

Ten recommended resources for challenging migration myths and racial stereotypes. Start with a clear explanation, then follow the original evidence.

Why these ten?

We chose trusted publishers, clear evidence and useful explanations, with a focus on Ireland. These are our recommendations for the community; there is no single best list for everyone.

The list includes guides to common myths, research, explanations of key words and practical ways to challenge racism. Each has a different use. We point out older information and research from other countries.

Check every source, including those you agree with. Claims need evidence. Everyone deserves respect, whatever their income or work.

  1. developmenteducation.ie

    2021 · Ireland & international · PDF

    10 Myths About Migration & Refugees (opens in a new tab)

    Start with common claims

    A clear teaching booklet with links to its sources by Tony Daly and Ciara Regan. Use its questions to examine migration claims and discuss the people behind the statistics.

    Background reading: its figures and asylum rules are from an earlier period. Use current sources below to check today’s figures and procedures.

  2. Irish Refugee Council

    Updated by the publisher · Ireland · Guides and advice

    Information Hub (opens in a new tab)

    Check the asylum process

    Guidance about seeking asylum — asking for protection because returning home would be unsafe. The hub provides different information for applications made before, or on or after, 12 June 2026.

    Choose the route that matches the application date. Follow the publisher’s updates; general information does not resolve an individual legal case.

  3. Economic and Social Research Institute

    Published March 2025 · Ireland · Research

    Monitoring Report on Integration 2024 (opens in a new tab)

    Check claims about settling into life in Ireland

    Irish surveys and public records about work, education, taking part in society and citizenship. A strong starting point for checking broad claims about migrants’ lives in Ireland.

    Check the year of each figure. “Foreign-born”, “non-Irish citizen” and “person seeking asylum” describe different groups.

  4. Irish Network Against Racism

    Guides and reports · Ireland · Anti-racism learning

    Learn About Racism in Ireland (opens in a new tab)

    Understand how racism works

    Definitions, learning materials and research from Ireland’s anti-racism network. Explains unfair assumptions about groups of people and how racism can occur in organisations as well as between individuals.

    Use this to learn about racism and find research. For population or migration figures, use the sources of figures listed here.

  5. European Migration Network / European Commission

    Updated by the publisher · European Union · What the short names mean

    EMN Asylum and Migration What the short names mean (opens in a new tab)

    Use precise words

    Explanations of migration and asylum terms in several languages. Check what a term means before comparing figures or repeating a claim that mixes different migration categories.

    Check whether a definition applies to the European Union or more widely. They do not determine a particular person’s immigration status.

  6. Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development

    November 2025 · OECD member countries · Research

    International Migration Outlook 2025 (opens in a new tab)

    Put migration figures in context

    Research comparing migration, work and settling into society across countries, with country summaries and tables of figures. Useful for testing sweeping international comparisons and claims about migrant workers.

    Use the country, migration category and data year attached to each table. An OECD average is not an Irish or local figure.

  7. University of Oxford · Migration Observatory

    Updated February 2026 · United Kingdom · Research summary

    The Labour Market Effects of Immigration (opens in a new tab)

    Look closely at jobs and wages

    Explains research on employment and wages, including why effects differ between groups and why measuring cause and effect is difficult. Links to the studies behind the analysis.

    UK evidence: useful for understanding the question, but it cannot establish the size of an effect in Saggart, Citywest or Ireland.

  8. EU Agency for Fundamental Rights

    2023 report · surveys carried out in 2021–2022 · 13 EU countries

    Being Black in the EU (opens in a new tab)

    Listen to evidence about discrimination

    Survey evidence on racism experienced by people of African descent, including respondents in Ireland. The report provides its questionnaire and technical information for readers to examine.

    The survey covers a particular group of people. These findings do not describe every migrant, every minority ethnic group or this local community.

  9. Council of Europe · Intercultural Cities

    2018 · International practice · PDF

    Antirumours Handbook (opens in a new tab)

    Build a community response

    A step-by-step guide to finding rumours, checking the evidence and helping residents challenge prejudice. Particularly useful for community groups planning ongoing work against racism.

    A practical guide with examples from earlier years. Use the sources of figures here for current migration figures.

  10. Council of Europe · Intercultural Cities

    2021 · International practice · PDF

    Claiming the Power of Dialogue (opens in a new tab)

    Have a helpful conversation

    A shorter toolkit for responding to stereotypes and misinformation in conversation. Covers listening, building a connection, correcting a claim and reflecting on what helped.

    Use alongside sound evidence and respect for people’s dignity. A conversation toolkit cannot verify a factual claim on its own.

Know the area

Local facts and figures from the CSO

Start with the Central Statistics Office’s local census tables, maps and publications. Use the Census 2022 reports below to explore all available topics.

Check which area a number describes

  • Saggart: the CSO’s town area (Built Up Area) and its wider statistical area (Electoral Division) have different boundaries.
  • Citywest: use the map to find the Small Areas — the small neighbourhood units used by the CSO — covering the place you mean. Do not relabel Saggart’s town total as a Citywest total.
  • South Dublin: county figures provide context. They are not figures for either neighbourhood; “Dublin” may refer to a wider area again.

Census 2022 describes 3 April 2022. It does not tell us how many people now live in accommodation provided by International Protection Accommodation Services (IPAS). Birthplace, citizenship, ethnicity and religion do not establish someone’s asylum or immigration status.

Browse the complete Census 2022 list of publications (opens in a new tab)
Income, housing and other local figures

These reports cover different areas and dates. Check which applies to you. An Eircode routing key (the first three characters of an Eircode) or a Local Electoral Area (used for council elections) covers more than one neighbourhood.

  • Geographical Profiles of Income in Ireland 2022 — Household Income (opens in a new tab)

    Published April 2025. Estimates for towns and wider statistical areas can help you explore Saggart and Citywest. The CSO calls this a Frontier Series report because it uses a newer approach. It combines official income records, but some people are not covered. Read the notes before comparing areas.

  • Geographical Profiles of Income — release collection (opens in a new tab)

    Access the 2016 and 2022 publications and their tables. Check whether the areas covered and the way figures were collected changed between years.

  • New Dwelling Completions (opens in a new tab)

    Reports every three months, with tables for council election areas and Eircode areas. These count newly finished homes, not the number of people moving into an area.

  • Residential Property Price Index and property prices by Eircode (opens in a new tab)

    The publication collection links to the interactive Eircode tool. Prices for an Eircode area cover a wider place. They do not tell you what every home in Saggart or Citywest is worth.

  • Residential Vacancy Based on Metered Electricity Consumption 2024 (opens in a new tab)

    Published March 2026. Includes South Dublin figures. This report uses electricity use to estimate empty homes, which differs from counting empty homes on census night. It does not count homes available to rent in each neighbourhood.

  • Census 2022 Electoral Division and health-area profiles (opens in a new tab)

    Choose the statistical area or health-service area you need. The area served by a health service may cross town boundaries.

  • Place of Work, School, College or Childcare census records (opens in a new tab)

    Explains how the CSO studies travel to work, school, college and childcare. Researchers need CSO approval to use the records, which have identifying details removed. For public information, use the employment and travel report and neighbourhood tables above.

CSO’s housing and household list of publications (opens in a new tab) provides further releases and updates. Check the area covered before treating national or regional figures as local.

Earlier census data and comparing change

Census 2016 Small Area Population Statistics (opens in a new tab) provides historical local tables. Census 2011 neighbourhood statistics (opens in a new tab) also offers downloadable historical tables; its old mapping software may no longer work. Use the CSO census collection (opens in a new tab) for other census years.

CSO introduced Built Up Areas in 2022. They are not directly comparable with the 2016 settlement boundaries. Before comparing years, check the area, the questions asked and who was counted. A larger boundary can make it look as if the population has grown.

Understand what you are seeing

Information, disinformation or misinformation?

Check the evidence, date and background first. You can check a claim even if you do not know why someone shared it.

Reliable information
Information you can check against evidence, with a clear source, date and explanation.
Misinformation
False or misleading information shared by someone who believes it is true.
Disinformation
False or misleading information made or shared on purpose to trick people, cause harm or gain an advantage.

A genuine photo can still mislead when given a false date or location. The Council of Europe also uses the word malinformation: genuine information used to cause harm. A mistake does not prove someone meant to mislead you. A claim that has not been checked is not necessarily false. The guides below explain more.

  1. Council of Europe

    2017 · Guide to key ideas

    Information Disorder (opens in a new tab)

    Understand the distinctions

    Claire Wardle and Hossein Derakhshan’s report explains how inaccurate or harmful information is created and shared, and why context and intention matter.

    An introduction to the main ideas. Its examples were written before today’s tools that use artificial intelligence (AI) to create text and images.

  2. UNESCO

    2018 handbook · Page updated 2024 · Multilingual

    Journalism, “Fake News” & Disinformation (opens in a new tab)

    Learn how to check information

    Lessons on misleading information, checking claims and finding reliable sources. Useful for educators, community communicators and anyone who wants a step-by-step approach.

    The checking skills are still useful. Examples of websites and apps reflect when the handbook was written.

  3. European Commission

    Updated by the publisher · European Union · Explainer

    Tackling Online Disinformation (opens in a new tab)

    Recognise deliberate deception

    An official explanation of misinformation and disinformation, the harms they can cause, and the EU’s response. A useful reference when the terms are used interchangeably.

    Explains the rules and key words. It does not check individual claims shared in local groups.

Build your checking skills

How can I tell what is real?

You do not need to be an expert to ask useful questions. These three resources help turn a moment of doubt into a practical checking habit.

  1. Media Literacy Ireland

    Ireland · Practical checklist

    Be Media Smart: tips (opens in a new tab)

    A quick everyday habit

    The Stop, Think, Check approach helps you pause, look beyond a headline, examine the source and check images and claims before sharing.

    A clear starting point for all ages and levels of digital confidence.

  2. Digital Inquiry Group

    Research-based learning · International use

    Civic Online Reasoning (opens in a new tab)

    Practise checking outside the page

    Lessons and activities on who is behind information, what evidence supports it and what other sources say. Open other websites to check who wrote a page and whether its claims are reliable.

    Developed in a US education context; the checking skills can be used with Irish sources too.

  3. Webwise · Irish Internet Safety Awareness Centre

    Ireland · Junior Cycle & educators

    Connected (opens in a new tab)

    Help young people check information online

    Teaching materials about news and false information, online wellbeing, data and digital rights. Includes English and Irish resources and a dedicated false-information module.

    Designed for teachers and young people; families and community educators can also explore the materials.

Before you share, try these five checks

  1. Pause. Notice if a post makes you feel angry, frightened or rushed. Read beyond its headline.
  2. Find the source. Who made the claim? Open the original report or statement, rather than relying on a screenshot.
  3. Check the context. Look for the date, place, definitions and comparison. Is an old story being presented as new?
  4. Look elsewhere. Check independent reporting or research. Several accounts copying the same post are not several independent sources.
  5. Check images and uncertainty. Look for an earlier version or use reverse image search. Appearance alone cannot prove authenticity. If you cannot verify a claim, do not share it as established fact.

These habits draw on Be Media Smart (opens in a new tab) and Civic Online Reasoning (opens in a new tab). Apply them to claims that support your own views too.
